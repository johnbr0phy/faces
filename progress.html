This round — what actually changed

The previous round lost 5–0 and the note from the critics was: stop adding geometry, change how the marks look. So nothing new was layered on. Six things were replaced.

  1. The nib. Strokes were butt-capped line segments at partial alpha, which is why everything read grey and seamed. A stroke is now a filled variable-width ribbon at full opacity — a solid wet core with loose filaments down each flank that catch and drop out on their own. Width comes from two octaves of pressure noise, ink pools at starts, stops and hard corners, and the nib lifts briefly where the paper tooth is high. Thin strokes skip more than fat ones.
  2. Doubled contours. Every head in the reference plates is two lines that touch and part, not one. doubled traces the whole contour a second time at a drifting offset.
  3. Hair owns the crown. It used to be a cap shape parked on top of the skull — a toupee. A mass is now built from two edges: a hairline pinned across the face, and the skull's own silhouette above it, pushed outward by the thickness of the hair. The head outline can no longer show through, and the mass wraps whatever the skull does under yaw. Combed and ribbed variants run stroke-by-stroke from the outer edge down to the hairline without crossing.
  4. Lumpy skulls. The outline was a convex hull, which can only ever be an egg. It is now the radial extent of the projected skull plus a per-seed lump profile on integer harmonics, so it can dent inward, and one optional flattened facet — a squared temple, a cut-off jaw.
  5. One heavy nose. Built on the face's projected vertical axis rather than raw 3D points, so it rides the skull through a turn instead of walking across the cheek. One unbroken run from between the brows to the tip, turning under into the nostril, at up to 1.5× the weight of anything else on the face.
  6. The body is drawn, not assembled. Gone: trapezoid chassis, ribbon tubes. The figure is one continuous silhouette — neck, shoulders, sides, legs, round the shoes — with the arms as their own shapes laid in front of it. Clothes cut into that: necklines, hems, cuffs, plackets, stripes clipped to the body.

Plus: every pen weight now scales with head size (small heads were reading as blobs), features are clamped inside the silhouette so nothing slides off on a hard turn, and the another dude button is written in the same ink as the drawing instead of typeset Georgia.

Still open

  • Nose variety — the long one dominates; the reference has more kinds of nose than we do.
  • Hatched hair is lighter than the reference's.
  • Mouths are still mostly a horizontal dash.
  • The body has no reference plate to be judged against, so it is judged on its own terms.
